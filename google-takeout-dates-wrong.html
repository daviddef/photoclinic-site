Answers

Why do my Google Takeout photos show the wrong date?

Short answer: Google stores the real date in a separate file, not in the photo. When you open the photo, your Mac shows the day you downloaded it.

Download for Mac

What is going on

When Google Photos exports your library, each photo comes with a small .json file beside it. The real date and time the photo was taken, the place and the caption are in that file. The photo itself often has none of them. So Finder, Apple Photos and other programs fall back to the date the file was created: the day you downloaded it.

Why time zones make it worse

Google stores times in a universal clock (UTC). Your photo was taken in your local time. If the two are mixed up, a photo can be hours out, and sometimes lands on the wrong day.

What fixes it

  • Read the date, place and caption from each photo's .json file.
  • Write them into the photo itself, so every program sees them.
  • Write local time with the right offset, not raw UTC.
  • Do this across all the zip files Google gave you, in one go.
  • Preview first: see what would change before anything does.

What if a photo has no date at all?

Some photos have no usable date. Photo Clinic rebuilds one from the file name (such as IMG_20190704_...), neighbouring photos or the folder name, and labels it as a guess with how sure it is. A guess never replaces a real date.

Always copy first. Photo Clinic makes the fixed copies in a new folder and leaves your Takeout alone. Keep the Takeout until you have checked the result.

How to see what date a photo really has

  • In Finder, select the photo and press Cmd+I. Created and Modified are the file's own dates, which are often the day you downloaded it. Under More Info, Content Created is the date stored inside the picture. If it is missing, the picture has no date inside.
  • In Apple Photos, select a photo and press Cmd+I to see the date Photos is using, and choose Image > Adjust Date and Time to change it for a few photos by hand.

The strange dates and what each means

  • The day you downloaded or unzipped them. The picture has no date inside, so the app used the file's own date.
  • 1 January 1970. A date of zero in the Unix clock. A tool wrote an empty date.
  • 1 January 2001. A date of zero in Apple's own clock. The same problem on a Mac.
  • Hours out, or a day out. A time in UTC was read as local time, or the other way round. See photo dates and time zones explained.
  • Videos wrong but photos right. Videos store their date in UTC, photos usually in local time, so a tool that treats both alike gets one of them wrong.

Can I fix it by hand?

For a handful of photos, yes: Photos' Adjust Date and Time. For thousands, no. A free command-line program called ExifTool can read the .json files and write the dates, but it needs careful setup, handles file names that were cut short poorly, and does nothing for time zones unless you tell it. That gap is what Photo Clinic fills. It reads the date from the JSON, writes local time with the right offset into a copy, and shows you a before-and-after for real photos first.
