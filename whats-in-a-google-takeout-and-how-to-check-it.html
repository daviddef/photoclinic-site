What is in a Google Takeout of Google Photos, and how to check you have it all

A Google Takeout arrives as a pile of zip files with strange names. Inside are your photos, and beside each one a small file of information. Here is what everything is, and how to be sure nothing is missing before you delete anything.

Last reviewed 26 October 2026. Written by the Photo Clinic team from what we learned moving large photo libraries. See how we write these guides.

The zip files

Google splits a large export into several archives. They are named like takeout-20241027T093500Z-001.zip, -002.zip and so on, in order. When you create the export you choose the archive size (for example 2 GB or 50 GB), so a big library can be dozens of zip files. If you chose to send them to Google Drive instead, they are files in a Drive folder, and Google emails you a list of links.

Two limits catch people out. The download links expire after a few days, and each archive can only be downloaded a limited number of times. Download everything promptly, and keep the zip files until you have checked the result.

The folders inside

  • Photos from 2019 (one folder per year): every photo and video taken that year, in a flat list.
  • Album folders: one folder for each album you made. The same photo can appear in a year folder and in an album, so a Takeout holds more files than you have pictures.
  • Trash, Archive and other folders may appear, depending on what you exported.

The small JSON file beside each photo

Next to each picture is a file ending in .json (in newer exports, .supplemental-metadata.json). It holds what Google knows about the photo: the date it was taken, the place, the description, the people, and whether you marked it as a favourite. The photo itself often has none of this. That is why photos from a Takeout can show the day you downloaded them, with no place or caption, until something reads the JSON files and writes the information back.

Why some names look cut short

Google limits the length of these file names, so a long photo name is shortened, and the matching JSON name is shortened too, in a way that does not always line up. A tool that just adds .json to the photo name will miss some. Photo Clinic matches by several rules, including the shortened forms, numbered duplicates like photo(1).jpg, and edited copies.

Edited copies, Live Photos and Motion Photos

  • An edit you made in Google Photos appears as a second file with -edited in the name, next to the original.
  • A Live Photo arrives as a still picture plus a separate short video with the same name.
  • A Motion Photo from some Android phones has a video hidden inside the picture file.

How to check that you have everything

  1. Count the zip files against the number in Google's email or the export page. Check the numbers run without gaps (001, 002, 003 …).
  2. Check each zip opens. A zip that stopped part-way will not open, or will report errors. Photo Clinic reads every zip and tells you which ones could not be read, so you can download only those again.
  3. Compare the picture count with what Google Photos shows (allowing for the extra album copies and the -edited files).
  4. Keep the originals until you have looked at the fixed copy and checked it in your photo app.
Photo Clinic never changes your zip files. It reads them, and writes the fixed photos into a new folder you choose.
